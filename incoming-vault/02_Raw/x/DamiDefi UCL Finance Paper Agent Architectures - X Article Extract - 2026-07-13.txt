Feed in data, get an output, make a decision. That is generation one thinking applied to generation three infrastructure. A March 2026 paper from UCL's Institute of Finance and Technology maps exactly why that approach is already obsolete and what the architecture looks like for systems that actually matter. The paper covers 83 papers across six research streams, proposes a four-layer architecture for financial AI agents, and identifies the specific deployment patterns that institutional finance is moving toward. I downloaded it. Fed it into Claude. Then used it as a blueprint to build three of the most accessible architectures it describes. Here is what the paper found and what I built from it. Why This Paper Is Different From Most AI Finance Research Most AI finance papers test whether a model can predict returns. This one asks a different question: how do you build an AI system that operates across the full workflow of financial decision-making, not just one component of it? The central argument is that the systemic implications of AI in finance depend less on model intelligence alone than on how agent architectures are distributed, coupled, and governed across financial institutions. That reframe matters. A better model improves one step. A better architecture changes the entire pipeline. The paper identifies three generations of financial AI: Generation 1 — Algorithmic finance: Rule-based execution. Speed and scale advantages. No interpretation of unstructured data. Generation 2 — Machine learning finance: Statistical pattern recognition. Better prediction on structured inputs. Still model-centric, not workflow-centric. Generation 3 — Agentic finance: Systems that perceive heterogeneous data, reason over objectives, generate action proposals, and interact with external tools in sequence. Workflow-centric rather than model-centric. The shift from generation two to generation three is not an incremental improvement. What is changing now is not simply that AI is becoming more accurate at prediction, but that it is beginning to operate across the full workflow of financial decision-making. Most retail traders and CT operators are still building generation one and two tools. The paper describes generation three. The Four-Layer Architecture Every Financial Agent Needs The paper's most useful contribution for builders is the four-layer architecture it proposes for financial AI agents. Every agent described in the survey maps to these four layers. Understanding the layers tells you exactly what your agent is missing. Layer 1: Data Perception What the agent can see. Structured data (prices, volumes, order book), unstructured data (news, filings, social media), and alternative data (satellite, credit card transactions, web traffic). Most agents built by retail traders only have structured data. The paper documents that institutional agents are increasingly ingesting all three simultaneously. Layer 2: Reasoning Engine How the agent processes what it sees. This is the LLM layer. The paper identifies retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) as the critical component here — the agent's ability to pull relevant context from a knowledge base before reasoning rather than relying on training data alone. An agent without RAG is reasoning from a fixed snapshot. An agent with RAG is reasoning from continuously updated context. Layer 3: Strategy Generation How the agent translates reasoning into proposals. This is where planning, hypothesis generation, and trade construction happen. The paper distinguishes between agents that generate a single recommendation and multi-agent systems where multiple strategy proposals are generated simultaneously and evaluated against each other. Layer 4: Execution and Control How the agent interacts with markets or external systems. The paper draws a critical distinction here between autonomy depth levels: Level 1: Agent produces analysis for human review. No autonomous action. Level 2: Agent executes within predefined parameters. Human sets the constraints. Level 3: Agent operates with full discretion within a mandate. Human oversight is periodic, not continuous. The paper's conclusion on autonomy: the most plausible near-term equilibrium is bounded autonomy, where AI agents operate as supervised co-pilots, monitoring systems, and constrained execution modules embedded within human decision processes. Level 2 is where most serious builders should be operating right now. Level 3 without robust governance creates systemic risk. The Six Application Domains the Paper Maps The paper covers six areas where financial AI agents are being deployed. These are not theoretical. They are documented from academic papers, institutional reports, and live deployments. 1. Autonomous Trading Multi-agent trading systems where specialised agents handle different functions. TradingAgents, documented in the paper, uses analyst agents, trader agents, and risk management agents in a coordinated pipeline. The analyst agents process news and market data. The trader agent constructs positions. The risk agent enforces limits. The key finding: multi-agent trading systems outperform single-agent systems on the same task because specialisation reduces the cognitive load on any individual component. An agent trying to do macro analysis, position sizing, and risk management simultaneously does all three worse than three agents each doing one. 2. Portfolio Management The paper documents the shift from single-model portfolio optimisation to multi-agent portfolio construction. The Ang et al. pipeline covered in the previous article in this series is the most advanced example documented. The general pattern: one agent per asset class, a construction agent, an adversarial agent, and a meta-agent that rewrites instructions after comparing forecasts to outcomes. 3. Risk Monitoring and Compliance The paper identifies this as the highest-adoption domain at the institutional level. Agents monitoring for anomalies, flagging regulatory violations, and generating compliance documentation are already in production at major institutions. The reason: this is Level 1 autonomy. The agent produces analysis for human review. No autonomous action. Regulatory approval is not required. 4. DeFi and On-Chain Intelligence The paper dedicates a full section to DeFi-specific agent applications. On-chain agents that monitor protocol health, flag liquidity risks, track whale movements, and generate alerts represent a domain where agentic AI has structural advantages over human monitoring. The data is public and machine-readable. The update frequency is too high for human processing. Agents are the natural solution. This is the most underbuilt domain relative to opportunity for CT operators specifically. 5. Research and Document Analysis Agents that process earnings calls, regulatory filings, and research reports at scale. The paper documents that financial LLMs fine-tuned on domain-specific data significantly outperform general-purpose models on financial text understanding tasks. BloombergGPT and FinGPT are the benchmarks. Claude with a domain-specific knowledge base loaded via Projects approaches similar performance for individual operators without the infrastructure cost. 6. Market Surveillance Agents monitoring for manipulation, unusual order patterns, and correlated behaviour across instruments. The paper identifies this as an area where AI agents have a speed advantage that cannot be matched by human teams regardless of size. The Three Architectures I Built The paper gives you a menu. I used it to build three architectures that are accessible with Claude today. Architecture 1: The RAG Research Agent This is the most immediately useful build for anyone doing financial research. It addresses the core limitation of using Claude without context: the agent is reasoning from training data rather than current information. The build uses Claude Projects with three components loaded as knowledge: A curated set of earnings transcripts, filings, and analyst reports for the assets you follow A set of macro data snapshots updated weekly A set of your own research notes and theses The prompt architecture: Prompt You are a financial research analyst with access to a curated knowledge base.Before answering any question, retrieve the most relevant documents from your knowledge base. Explicitly cite which documents inform your answer. Flag if the answer requires information not present in the knowledge base.When analysing any asset: 1. What does the current filing data say about fundamental position? 2. What does the macro context suggest about the sector environment? 3. Where do my existing research notes conflict with the current data? 4. What is the single most important question this analysis leaves unanswered?Do not produce a summary. Produce a structured analysis with explicit source citations. The difference from standard Claude usage: the agent is not reasoning from training data. It is reasoning from your specific knowledge base and telling you when it cannot find the answer there. That distinction changes the reliability of every output. Architecture 2: The Multi-Agent Trading Review System This implements the paper's finding that specialised agents outperform generalist agents on complex tasks. It uses three agents in sequence, each with a narrow mandate. Agent 1 — The Data Perception Agent Prompt You are a data perception agent. Your only job is to collect and structure information. Do not interpret. Do not recommend. Only gather and organise.For [ASSET], collect and structure the following: - Price action: current price, 30-day change, distance from 52-week high and low - Volume: current vs 30-day average, any unusual volume events in the last 5 days - News: any material announcements in the last 7 days with dates - Sentiment: current social sentiment signal if availableOutput as a structured data block. No analysis. No interpretation. This output feeds a reasoning agent that follows. Agent 2 — The Reasoning Agent Prompt You are a reasoning agent. You receive structured data from a perception agent. Your job is to interpret that data and generate hypotheses.Here is the structured data: [PASTE AGENT 1 OUTPUT]Generate three hypotheses about the current situation: Hypothesis 1: The bullish interpretation of this data Hypothesis 2: The bearish interpretation of this data Hypothesis 3: The interpretation that neither the bulls nor bears are currently pricingFor each hypothesis, state: the evidence supporting it, the evidence contradicting it, and the single data point that would confirm or refute it.Do not give a recommendation. Generate hypotheses for the strategy agent that follows. Agent 3 — The Strategy Agent Prompt You are a strategy agent. You receive hypotheses from a reasoning agent. Your job is to translate the most credible hypothesis into a structured trade proposal.Here are the hypotheses: [PASTE AGENT 2 OUTPUT]Select the hypothesis with the best evidence-to-risk ratio. State why you selected it over the alternatives.Then produce a structured trade proposal: - Directional bias and rationale - Entry condition (what must be true before entering) - Invalidation condition (what would make this hypothesis wrong) - Position sizing recommendation as a percentage of portfolio - Time horizonDo not factor in execution. A human makes the final decision. Architecture 3: The DeFi Monitoring Agent This implements the paper's on-chain intelligence section. It addresses the specific structural advantage agents have in DeFi: data is public, machine-readable, and updates too frequently for human monitoring. Prompt You are a DeFi protocol monitoring agent.Search the web for current data on [PROTOCOL NAME] across the following dimensions:1. Liquidity health — Current TVL and 7-day change — Any unusual liquidity withdrawal events in the last 48 hours — Concentration of top 10 liquidity providers as a percentage of total2. Protocol risk signals — Any governance proposals passed or pending that affect tokenomics or fee structure — Any smart contract upgrades or migrations announced or deployed in the last 30 days — Any oracle dependency changes or price feed anomalies flagged in the last 7 days3. Whale and large wallet activity — Any wallets holding more than 1% of circulating supply that have moved tokens in the last 7 days — Direction of movement: accumulation or distribution4. Competitive context — TVL change relative to the three closest competing protocols in the last 30 days — Any narrative or product development at competitors that could draw liquidity awayOutput as a structured monitoring report with a risk flag rating: Green (no material signals), Amber (watch closely), Red (requires immediate review)This report is reviewed by a human before any action is taken. The Systemic Risk Finding You Need to Know About The paper has a section on systemic implications that most people writing about it skip. It matters for anyone building or using these systems. The paper introduces five parameters that determine how much systemic risk an agentic finance deployment creates: Autonomy depth — how much discretion the agent has without human approval Model heterogeneity — whether different institutions are using different models or the same ones Execution coupling — how tightly the agent is connected to live execution systems Infrastructure concentration — how many institutions depend on the same underlying providers Supervisory observability — how visible the agent's decision process is to regulators The concern: if multiple institutions deploy similar agents using similar underlying models and similar data sources, their behaviour will correlate. Correlated automated behaviour across institutions during a stress event amplifies volatility rather than absorbing it. The Flash Crash of 2010 is the documented precedent. Agentic AI at scale creates the conditions for larger versions of that event. This does not mean do not build. It means build with bounded autonomy. Every agent in this article operates at Level 1 or Level 2. A human reviews before any action is taken. That constraint is not caution. It is the correct architecture for the current moment.
