## The Robotics Thesis

You've probably seen this chart floating around on the timeline recently.

Robotics investments from VCs just hit a new quarterly high (~$16B and climbing).

This is over double what venture capitalists invested in Q1 of this year, and once you understand why, you'll also understand why this liquidity isn't slowing down anytime soon.

For perspective, robotics VC investments are only 1/14 of what is currently being poured into AI, and ultimately, this gap is where I think the asymmetric opportunity for investors lies right now.

So what's actually driving VC interest?

To understand the robotics thesis, you can't just think about this sector as "building robots."

This sector serves more as a proxy for bridging AI progress to the real world.

For the last decade, AI progress has been almost entirely confined to a screen.

i.e., models have gotten smarter, but they obviously can't pick anything up, walk anywhere, or interact with the physical world.

That gap is where the entire robotics thesis lives, and I believe we are now entering the beginning of the exponential growth curve for this sector:

Robotics constraints are starting to break

The bear case on robotics for the last five years has primarily been the same two things:

1. AI hasn't been good enough

1. The hardware has been too expensive & too hard to manufacture at scale

Both of these constraints are beginning to break.

On capability: Robotics is currently at an inflection point for reaching real-world field deployment.

Three years ago, the robotics field looked completely different.

In 2022, Google's RT-1 introduced robot learning, trained on 130,000 demonstrations across 700+ narrow tasks.

In 2023, RT-2 introduced the first Vision-Language-Action model.

And by 2025, that progress had translated into large-scale production deployment at companies like Tesla and Figure AI.

In just three years, the field went from narrow, single-task demo learning to factory-scale deployment.

If we compare this to LLM progression, robotics is only a few years behind.

To make an apples-to-apples comparison, we can translate the current state of robotics capabilities to roughly that of GPT-2.

Capable but still lacking in real-world field deployment.

That gap is starting to close fast, and @Figure_robot recently ran a livestream that proves it.

Their F.03 humanoid robots sorted packages (fully autonomously) for 160+ consecutive hours.

This kind of hard proof of robotics progression did not exist a year ago.

There are many such examples of practical leaps in robotic capabilities over the past year, but the TL;DR is that real-world field deployment is finally coming into the picture.

On cost: Humanoid robotics costs have been rapidly decreasing.

Overall robot manufacturing costs

- 2020: $1M+ research platforms

- 2022/2023: $150,000-$500,000 per unit

- 2026: $30,000-$150,000 per unit (depending on capability)

And from here, this cost curve is only expected to drop further.

By 2030, average selling prices are expected to fall ~70% to a new industry average of only $37,000.

Practical example

@weaverobotics just announced its viral Issac 1 robot for only $8000 - pricing we've never seen before.

This is the same kind of cost curve that took solar panels and EV batteries from niche to mainstream in under a decade.

And the perfect storm for robotics is brewing right now.

Robotics/AI capabilities are rapidly improving, while costs are simultaneously dropping.

These are some of the underlying reasons for massive VC interest right now, and if you're a smart investor, you'll want to get on this train while liquidity is still pouring in.

## How To Get Exposure

Everything discussed below is not financial advice, and is my opinion only. In the last section, I'll hand you some resources to help with your own due diligence/research.

There are five layers worth knowing when it comes to building a robotics portfolio.

As we get deeper into each layer, note that the risk appetite also increases.

I'm personally allocating 5-10% of my portfolio to a mix of these layers to build a long-term, high conviction robotics portfolio, but feel free to structure yours as you'd like.

Layer One: ETFs

If you want exposure to robotics without picking individual winners, ETFs are where you start. They give you diversified coverage across the ecosystem and let the sector do the work rather than betting on individual companies.

There are three robotics ETFs you should know about:

1. $BOTZ: Global X Robotics and Artificial Intelligence ETF

$BOTZ is the fund most retail investors reach for first when they hear "robotics ETF."

It tracks 68 companies across areas like industrial robotics, automation, non-industrial robots, and autonomous vehicles.

$BOTZ returned ~30% over the past year and is up roughly 10% year to date.

The trade-off here is concentration: a handful of Japanese and Swiss automation giants drive the bulk of returns, so it behaves more like a focused growth fund than a broad-theme play.

2.    $ROBO: ROBO Global Robotics and Automation Index ETF

ROBO holds 91 companies with a 0.95% expense ratio, the most expensive on this list, but it earns it through a highly specialized index that focuses purely on robotics.

3.     $ARKQ: ARK Autonomous Technology and Robotics ETF

This fund has significant exposure to Tesla, and is only worth owning if you share Cathie Wood's specific vision for the future of autonomy. It also has meaningful defense exposure, with Kratos Defense and AeroVironment among its top holdings.

Layer Two: Large Cap Public Equities

This is where you get direct exposure to specific companies already generating real robotics revenue.

Some companies worth keeping on your watchlist:

1. $TSLA - Tesla

Tesla is the highest-conviction, highest-risk name in the entire robotics space. Elon Musk has stated directly that roughly 80% of Tesla's future value will come from Optimus.

Wedbush's Dan Ives calls Tesla "the best physical AI company in the world" and has predicted a potential $2 trillion market cap by the end of 2026, based primarily on growth in FSD and robotics.

2.     $AMZN - Amazon

Amazon is the most underrated robotics play in large-cap tech.

Amazon has deployed over 1 million robots since 2012, running a full ecosystem of systems. I suspect that their robotics success will bolster the company's overall productivity and revenue margins.

Of course, there are plenty more large caps to add here, but these are the two I think everyone should have on their radar.

Layer Three: Pick-and-Shovels

During the gold rush, it wasn't the miners who got rich. It was the people supplying the equipment for them to mine who made a fortune.

That same framework applies here - the companies that get rich over the next decade will be the ones selling/producing the resources needed for robotics companies to succeed.

There are many pick-and-shovel sectors worth mentioning, but here is a list:

• Compute
• Vision
• Actuation
• Simulation
• Semiconductors

and more

Layer Four: Pure-Play Robotics Stocks

This is where the thesis gets more concentrated. These are companies whose entire business models live or die on robotics.

Examples:

1. $OUST - Ouster

Ouster is the picks-and-shovels play for robot perception.

Every robot that navigates the real world needs eyes, and lidar is increasingly that layer.

2.     $SYM - Symbotic

Symbotic is a play on the warehouse-automation wave (not directly on humanoid robots specifically).

The thesis is: Every major retailer and grocer in the world still runs distribution centers that are largely manual. Labor is expensive, unreliable, and increasingly hard to hire. Symbotic builds fully automated warehouse systems that replace the manual layer.

They already have huge clients like Walmart and a mass deployment roadmap in the works.

Layer Five: High Risk Betas

Lastly, layer five is where you can consider taking higher-risk bets.

Think: Crypto x robotics projects, early-stage startup investing (if applicable), and so on.

One ticker worth researching is $BOT - RoboStrategy. Probably one of the most-hyped companies on the timeline right now, and it's because they allow you to gain access to private robotics companies.

I personally believe this layer is where some of the most asymmetric opportunities live, precisely because nobody is looking here yet.
