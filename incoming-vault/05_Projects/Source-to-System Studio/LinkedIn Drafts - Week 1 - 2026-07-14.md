# Draft 1 — Monday: scattered business knowledge

**Hook**

A business can have the right information and still be unable to use it.

**Draft**

The knowledge is usually there — in inboxes, PDFs, old quotes, spreadsheets, WhatsApp messages and one person's memory.

The problem is not always a lack of information. It is that the information is not organised around the decisions the business needs to make.

A useful first step is to map:

- what sources exist;
- which source is trusted for which question;
- where information is duplicated or missing; and
- which repeated decision is costing the most time.

Only then does it make sense to build a context system, workflow or dashboard.

At Source-to-System Studio, we start with the smallest useful system — not another dashboard for its own sake.

If your business has good knowledge in the wrong shape, that is usually a solvable problem.

**CTA:** What business question do you still answer manually every week?

# Draft 2 — Wednesday: trade and supply businesses

**Hook**

A quote can be technically correct and still be slow, inconsistent and hard to hand over.

**Draft**

Trade and supply businesses often carry product knowledge across catalogues, supplier PDFs, spreadsheets, old quotes and conversations with experienced staff.

That creates predictable friction:

- the same product question gets answered repeatedly;
- quote assumptions are not visible to the next person;
- product substitutions are hard to compare; and
- corrections happen late in the customer conversation.

A practical quote-intelligence system does not need to automate everything.

It can begin with:

1. a structured product and specification index;
2. explicit pricing and substitution rules;
3. reusable quote components; and
4. a review checklist for the exceptions that still need human judgement.

The goal is not to replace expertise. It is to make expertise easier to find, reuse and check.

**CTA:** Where does quoting slow down in your business — finding information, pricing, or review?

# Draft 3 — Friday: proof before promotion

**Hook**

The strongest business case study is not "we used AI." It is "this repeated problem changed in a measurable way."

**Draft**

Before a pilot begins, define the baseline:

- hours spent on the repeated task;
- rework or correction frequency;
- unanswered customer questions;
- handover friction; and
- the decisions that remain slow or uncertain.

Then build one bounded improvement, review it with the business owner and measure what changed.

That produces better proof than a generic testimonial because it shows:

- what the starting problem was;
- what system was built;
- what the system does not claim; and
- which result was actually observed.

For our own pilots, names, logos, testimonials and metrics remain private until the business gives written approval for each use.

That boundary matters. Good proof should be useful without becoming exaggerated proof.

**CTA:** What would you want measured before calling a business improvement successful?
